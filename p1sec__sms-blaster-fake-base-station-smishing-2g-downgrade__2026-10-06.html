英文原标题：SMS blasters explained: how fake base stations deliver smishing outside the network

An SMS blaster is a portable fake base station built for one job: pushing mass phishing text messages straight to nearby phones over the radio interface, without ever touching the operator's SMS infrastructure. It typically works by luring handsets onto a rogue 2G cell, where the network is not authenticated to the phone, and then injecting SMS that look like they came from a bank, a parcel service or a government agency. Because the messages never cross the SMSC, the SMS firewall or the interconnect, the controls operators have spent a decade building against smishing simply do not see them. What changed in 2025 and 2026 is scale and geography. Publicly reported police cases, which first clustered in Southeast Asia, now include Europe and North America: Toronto Police announced Canada's first known SMS blaster arrests in May 2026 after a campaign that had run since November 2025, and Austrian police arrested a suspect in Vienna the same month with a device in the boot of a car near a major public event. This article explains how SMS blasters work at the protocol level, why they sit outside traditional SMS defences, what they do to the legitimate network around them, and what operators, regulators and device makers can realistically do about them. What is an SMS blaster? An SMS blaster is a rogue base station, usually a software-defined radio with a power amplifier and a laptop, that impersonates a legitimate cell in order to deliver bulk SMS to every phone that camps on it. It is a close relative of the IMSI catcher, but its purpose is fraud delivery rather than surveillance, and it is often driven around in a car or carried in a backpack so it can move through dense urban areas and reach as many handsets as possible. The technique is not new in principle. BTS impersonation has been documented for as long as GSM has existed, and the underlying weakness is the one described in our analysis of fake base station attacks in 5G : a phone has to decide which cell to trust before any authentication has taken place. What is new is the commercialisation. Criminal groups now buy ready-made blaster kits, pair them with phishing-as-a-service platforms, and operate them as a delivery channel in the same way they would rent a bulk SMS route. From a defender's point of view, the important distinction is between an SMS blaster and an SMS campaign sent through the network. A conventional smishing campaign uses A2P aggregators, SIM farms or compromised accounts, and its traffic flows through the operator's core where it can be filtered. An SMS blaster bypasses the core entirely. The message is generated on the rogue cell and delivered over the air, so from the operator's network the subscriber never received anything at all.
