You Had Me at Hello The Rise of the Wrong Number Text Scam “You had me at hello” may be one of the most famous lines in cinema. Unfortunately, many victims of conversational scams, dragged into a scheme by a simple wrong-number text saying “Hello,” are also being had. Since October is Cybersecurity Awareness Month , this is a good time for a quick reminder of how scammers use social-engineered conversations to build the trust needed to pull off scams. Conversational SMS fraud has risen sharply in recent years, reflecting a shift from blunt bulk scams to more sophisticated social engineering through messaging channels. To understand why a simple wrong-number text can be such an effective scam-opening tactic today, it helps to understand why SMS scams evolved from blunt bulk scams to sophisticated social engineering schemes, and how AI enables scammers to scale. From Bulk to Conversational Scams When Renee Zellweger’s character, Dorothy, said her famous words in Jerry Maguire , SMS had just launched, and the average user received fewer than five text messages a year. Smishing and other SMS scams were nowhere on the horizon. A few years into the 2000s, SMS adoption had taken off . As text messaging spread, scammers began exploiting it. Because SMS was still a relatively new channel, people had little awareness of SMS scams or how they worked. In general, people trusted text messages, which made scams more likely to succeed. Scammers exploited this by sending mass texts that directed victims to premium-rate numbers, fake prize offers, and malicious websites. Techniques such as spoofing the sender ID to make messages appear to come from a trusted sender made scams even more believable and paved the way for banking scams. Overall, early SMS scams were unsophisticated and indiscriminate, prioritizing scale over response rate and relying on harvesting a small portion of a large target base. Two developments have since made blunt SMS scams less effective. First, people today are generally much more aware of cyber risks and scams in digital channels, including in messaging services. Second, most MNOs have deployed SMS firewalls to block unsolicited text messages. The effectiveness of these varies, with some only blocking the most rudimentary spam and others being very accurate, even when advanced bypass techniques are used. Nevertheless, scammers have more difficulty getting their messages through to victims today compared to ten or fifteen years ago. As firewall technology has improved and subscriber awareness has increased, scammers have been forced to refine their methods. It is in this light that we need to understand conversational scams and why it has emerged as a significant threat in the last few years. Conversational scams are unsolicited messages designed to start a dialogue rather than deliver immediate monetization.
