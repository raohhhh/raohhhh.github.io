英文原标题：CAMEL security in mobile networks: what CAP, CSI and the gsmSCF expose

CAMEL is the mechanism that lets a home network keep control of a subscriber's calls, data sessions and messages while that subscriber is attached to somebody else's network. It is why a prepaid customer can roam abroad and still be charged in real time, why an outbound call made in a foreign country can be routed back through the home operator, and why a number of service features survive the move from the home PLMN to a visited one. It does this by shipping a small piece of the home network's service logic into the visited network's call handling, and then having the visited network call home whenever that logic is triggered. That design is useful and it is also a trust relationship expressed in signalling. The visited network acts on subscriber data it receives over MAP, and the home network accepts a service dialogue opened by a switch it does not operate, across an interconnect shared with hundreds of other parties. CAMEL is much less discussed than SS7 , Diameter or GTP, largely because it is a service layer rather than a transport, and it tends to be owned by charging and value added services teams rather than by security. This article sets out what CAMEL actually is, where its trust boundaries sit, and what a signalling assessment should establish about a deployment. What CAMEL is and why it is still running CAMEL stands for Customised Applications for Mobile network Enhanced Logic. It is the mobile network's adaptation of the fixed line Intelligent Network concept, in which the switch handling a call pauses at defined points and asks an external service control node what to do next. The service description lives in 3GPP TS 22.078, the architecture and procedures in TS 23.078, and the protocol itself, the CAMEL Application Part or CAP, in TS 29.078. CAP is derived from INAP and runs over TCAP, which in turn runs over SCCP, so in a modern deployment it travels the same SIGTRAN stack as MAP does. CAMEL is specified in phases, conventionally numbered one to four, each adding capability: basic call control first, then more detailed charging and mid-call control, then GPRS and short message triggers, then finer control over call parties and follow-on behaviour. Which phase a given roaming relationship supports is negotiated, and mismatched phase support between two operators is a routine operational issue rather than an exotic one. It persists because the services built on it persist. Prepaid roaming is the obvious case, but CAMEL also underpins home routing of outbound calls, closed user groups, various number translation and screening services, and a range of operator specific features that were built over a decade and never migrated. As 2G and 3G are switched off, the circuit switched footprint that CAMEL serves shrinks, and we looked at what that transition leaves behind in our piece on the phasing out of 2G and 3G .
