英文原标题：Steering of Roaming (SoR) security: how network selection control rides on signalling

Steering of Roaming (SoR) is the mechanism a home operator uses to influence which visited network its subscribers register on when they roam abroad. It is primarily a commercial control, used to push roamers onto preferred partners where the home operator has better wholesale rates or quality agreements. What makes it relevant to security is how it is implemented: SoR rides on the same SS7 and Diameter signalling that carries roaming registration, which means it shares the interconnect trust problems of those protocols and introduces a few of its own. GSMA IR.73 is the implementation reference, and understanding it clarifies both how SoR works and where its risks sit. What Steering of Roaming is When a subscriber powers on abroad, the device scans for available visited networks and attempts to register on one. Steering of Roaming is the home operator's ability to influence that choice so the subscriber ends up on a preferred partner rather than whichever network the device would otherwise pick. The home operator gets value from this because roaming wholesale costs and quality differ between partners; steering directs traffic to the relationships the operator wants to use. GSMA IR.73, the Steering of Roaming Implementation Guidelines, defines how operators should implement SoR with minimal impact on visited networks, including how it interacts with the SS7 and Diameter signalling links involved in registration. The guidelines exist precisely because SoR works by interfering, in a controlled way, with the normal network-selection and registration flow, and that interference has to be bounded so it does not degrade the roaming experience or the visited network. How SoR works over signalling Roaming registration is a signalling conversation. In 2G and 3G it runs over SS7 using MAP, where a visited network sends an Update Location toward the subscriber's home network and the home network responds. In 4G the equivalent exchange runs over Diameter. SoR techniques operate by influencing this exchange so that registration on a non-preferred network is made to fail or look unattractive, prompting the device to try another network, while registration on a preferred network succeeds normally. Because the home network sits at one end of every registration for its own subscribers, it is in a position to apply this influence. The mechanics differ between SS7 and Diameter, and between approaches that act in the signalling plane versus those that use over-the-air SIM configuration, but the common thread is that SoR manipulates the registration outcome rather than the radio. That is the key technical point: SoR is a signalling-layer control, and it lives on the same interconnect surface discussed in our telecom interconnection security overview, and in our look at the HLR that anchors subscriber state on the home side.
