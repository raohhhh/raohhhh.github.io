英文原标题：Lawful interception security in 5G: protecting the system that is built to listen

Lawful interception is a licence condition for nearly every mobile operator, and in 5G it is specified in unusual detail. 3GPP TS 33.126 sets the requirements, TS 33.127 defines the architecture and functions, and TS 33.128 fixes the protocols and procedures down to the encoding of individual records. Between them, those documents tell an operator what must be intercepted, which network functions perform the interception, and how the output reaches a law enforcement monitoring facility. What they cannot answer is the question that matters most to a security team: the interception system is, by construction, a sanctioned mechanism for covertly copying subscriber signalling and content, so it concentrates more risk in a single place than almost anything else an operator runs. That is the framing of this article. Not lawful interception as a compliance exercise, but lawful interception as an asset that has to be defended to the same standard as the subscriber database it can read from. The controls that matter are mostly architectural: strict separation between the interception control plane and normal network operations, cryptographic protection of the internal interfaces, provisioning paths that cannot be driven from ordinary OAM systems, and audit trails that a network engineer cannot quietly edit. Operators that treat lawful interception as somebody else's regulatory problem tend to end up with a privileged, poorly monitored subsystem sitting inside the core, and that is exactly the shape of thing that sophisticated intruders look for. What lawful interception is in 3GPP terms Lawful interception is the standardised capability that lets an operator deliver a specific subscriber's communications, and information about those communications, to an authorised law enforcement agency under a legal warrant. 3GPP splits the specification across three documents so that requirements, architecture and protocol detail can evolve separately. TS 33.126 states the requirements: what has to be interceptable, what the target identifiers can be, what has to remain invisible to the target and to network staff, and what timeliness and completeness obligations apply. TS 33.127 turns those requirements into an architecture of named functions and interfaces. TS 33.128 defines the stage 3 detail, including the actual record structures and protocol bindings, so that an operator and an agency can interoperate without bilateral engineering. The 4G and pre-5G equivalents are TS 33.106, TS 33.107 and TS 33.108, and many networks still run both generations of the architecture side by side because interception obligations do not disappear when a technology is in run-down. Alongside 3GPP, ETSI Technical Committee LI produces the specifications that much of the world actually deploys, including the TS 103 221 series for the internal interfaces used to administer interception and carry its output, and the TS 102 232 series for handover delivery over IP.
