英文原标题：SIM OTA security: how over-the-air SIM management works and where it fails

SIM over-the-air (OTA) management is the mechanism that lets a mobile operator change what sits on a subscriber's SIM after the card has left the factory: updating files such as preferred roaming lists, installing or removing applets, rotating keys, and triggering SIM Toolkit applications. It works by wrapping commands in secured packets defined by ETSI and 3GPP and delivering them, most often, as binary SMS that the handset passes straight to the card without the user seeing anything. SIM OTA security is therefore the question of who can get such a packet accepted by a card, and what that packet is then allowed to do. The short answer is that OTA is only as strong as three things: the keys and algorithms the card enforces for each application, the minimum security level each application demands, and the network's willingness to deliver SIM data download messages from sources that have no business sending them. Public disclosures over the last decade have shown failures in all three. This article explains how the OTA chain is built, where it breaks, and how operators can test and harden it without waiting for the next disclosure. It extends our earlier work on SIM man in the middle experimentation and on SIM types, attacks and penetration testing . What SIM OTA is and why operators depend on it SIM OTA is the remote administration channel between an operator's OTA platform and the UICC in a subscriber's device. Operators depend on it because the SIM is a long-lived, physically distributed asset: once millions of cards are in the field, the only practical way to change their contents is remotely. Typical OTA use cases are mundane but operationally important: updating preferred roaming partner lists and the forbidden PLMN list, changing operator menus, and managing SIM Toolkit applications. On Java Card and GlobalPlatform cards, OTA is also how applets are loaded, installed or deleted and how card content management keys are rotated. The same properties that make OTA useful make it sensitive. An OTA command is executed by the card, not by the handset operating system, so it sits below most of the protections a user or device vendor can apply. A command that the card accepts can read or modify files, and a SIM Toolkit application that the card triggers can issue proactive commands to the handset, for example to send an SMS, provide local information such as the serving cell, or open a browser session. That is why the integrity of the OTA chain matters to subscriber privacy as much as to service management, and why it belongs in any discussion of subscriber identity and its protection . How the OTA chain is built: from platform to card The OTA chain has four links: the OTA platform in the operator's network, the bearer that carries the command, the handset that forwards it, and the card application that executes it.
