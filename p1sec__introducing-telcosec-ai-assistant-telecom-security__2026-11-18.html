Why telecom security needs its own assistant Mobile network security sits between two engineering cultures. Telecom engineers know the protocols, the network functions and the interconnect agreements. Security engineers know threat models, detection and remediation. Most questions that matter, such as whether a MAP message pattern is reconnaissance or normal roaming traffic, need both views at once. General-purpose AI models can talk fluently about SS7 or Diameter , but they answer from whatever they absorbed in training, without telecom security sources to check against. A confident answer that cannot show where it comes from is a risk in a field where one wrong filtering rule can open an interconnect to location tracking. TelcoSec AI was built to bridge telecom engineering and security engineering, and to show its sources while it does. What an answer looks like TelcoSec AI reads before it writes. For each question it retrieves the relevant material, ranks what matters for your case, then answers in a fixed structure: A diagnosis or a plan. What the behaviour you describe most likely means, or what a rollout should cover. Actions. What to test, what to block and what to monitor. Sources. Each cited point shows the source behind it, and its type: a P1 vulnerability entry, a guideline or a specification clause. General practice, marked apart. Advice that comes from general knowledge rather than a cited source is labelled as such, so you can tell the two apart at a glance. Conversations keep their context, so a follow-up such as "is our own network exposed, and how would we check?" builds on the previous answer instead of starting again.
