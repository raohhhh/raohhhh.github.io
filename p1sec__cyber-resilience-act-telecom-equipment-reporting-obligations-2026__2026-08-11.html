英文原标题：Cyber Resilience Act and telecom equipment: what changes on 11 September 2026

From 11 September 2026, manufacturers of products with digital elements sold in the European Union must report actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe security incidents to ENISA and a national CSIRT, with an early warning inside 24 hours. That deadline is the first hard obligation of Regulation (EU) 2024/2847, the Cyber Resilience Act, and it lands well before the bulk of the regulation applies on 11 December 2027. For telecom, this matters more than most sectors realise, because routers, modems, switches and network management systems are named explicitly in the regulation's list of important products, and because the vulnerability-handling practices the CRA assumes are not the ones the mobile industry has historically run on. The mobile ecosystem already has a security assurance scheme in NESAS and SCAS, and a mature set of GSMA baseline documents. Neither of those is a substitute for the CRA. NESAS and SCAS test whether a product meets a set of security requirements at a point in time. The CRA is a product regulation with market-surveillance teeth, a CE marking pathway, and continuous obligations across the whole support period of a product. This post sets out what the CRA covers, which telecom equipment sits where in its risk classes, what changes on 11 September 2026 versus 11 December 2027, and what operators and network equipment vendors should be doing about it now. It is written for procurement, product security and network security teams, not for lawyers. What the Cyber Resilience Act actually regulates The CRA regulates products with digital elements placed on the EU market. It is not a network operator obligation in the way NIS2 is, and it is not a data protection regime. It sits on the product side: whoever manufactures, imports or distributes hardware or software with a digital element carries obligations about how that product is designed, how its vulnerabilities are handled, and how long it is supported. The regulation entered into force on 10 December 2024. Its substantive requirements apply from 11 December 2027, with two earlier milestones: the rules for notified bodies apply from 11 June 2026, and the reporting obligations in Article 14 apply from 11 September 2026. The staggered structure is deliberate. The Commission wanted incident and vulnerability visibility to start flowing before the full conformity assessment machinery is running. Two clusters of requirements matter for anyone who builds or buys telecom infrastructure. The first is the essential cybersecurity requirements in Annex I Part I, which cover things like shipping without known exploitable vulnerabilities, secure default configuration, protection of confidentiality and integrity of data, minimisation of attack surface, and the ability to distribute security updates.
