英文原标题：Public warning system security: how cell broadcast alerts are spoofed and what operators control

Public warning system (PWS) messages are the one kind of traffic a mobile network is designed to deliver to every handset in an area, attached or not, subscribed or not, and without asking the device to authenticate anything. That design choice is deliberate: an earthquake, tsunami or flood warning has to reach a visitor with a foreign SIM, a phone with no credit and a device that has only just been switched on. The consequence is that, in commercial deployments today, a handset has no standardised cryptographic way to tell a genuine cell broadcast alert from one transmitted by a rogue base station. This article explains how ETWS, CMAS, EU-Alert and the other regional services ride on the 3GPP cell broadcast machinery in 4G and 5G, where the real attack surface sits (on the air interface and in the core), what 3GPP SA3 has and has not standardised, and what an operator can actually control and test. The short answer: the radio side is largely outside the operator's hands, but the chain from the alerting authority to the Cell Broadcast Centre and on to the AMF or MME is operator infrastructure, and it deserves the same scrutiny as any other critical signalling path. What the public warning system is in 3GPP terms PWS is the 3GPP umbrella for regional warning services that share one delivery mechanism: cell broadcast. The service requirements are set in 3GPP TS 22.268, and the technical realisation of the Cell Broadcast Service across 2G, 3G, 4G and 5G is specified in TS 23.041. Several national and regional services map onto that common layer: the Earthquake and Tsunami Warning System (ETWS) designed for Japan, the Commercial Mobile Alert System (CMAS) that underpins Wireless Emergency Alerts in the United States, the Korean Public Alert System (KPAS), and EU-Alert in Europe. In Europe the regulatory driver is Article 110 of the European Electronic Communications Code, which required Member States to have public warnings transmitted by providers of mobile number based interpersonal communications services by 21 June 2022. Many Member States met that obligation with cell broadcast, which means EU-Alert is no longer a niche feature switched on for tests; it is production infrastructure with a legal mandate behind it and a civil protection authority depending on it. The reason cell broadcast was chosen over SMS is capacity and reach. An SMS campaign to millions of subscribers loads the signalling network and delivers one message per subscriber. A cell broadcast message is transmitted once per cell and received by every capable device in idle or connected mode, including inbound roamers. That same broadcast property is what makes the security problem interesting. How a warning travels from the authority to the handset A warning passes through three segments: the alerting authority's system, the operator's cell broadcast core, and the radio access network.
