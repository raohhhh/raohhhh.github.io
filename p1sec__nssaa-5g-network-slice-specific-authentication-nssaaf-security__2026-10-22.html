英文原标题：NSSAA in 5G: securing network slice-specific authentication and the NSSAAF

Network slice-specific authentication and authorisation (NSSAA) is the 5G mechanism that lets a slice owner decide, with its own credentials and its own AAA server, whether a device may use a particular network slice. It was introduced in 3GPP Release 16, runs after primary authentication, and is specified in TS 23.501, TS 23.502 and clause 16 of TS 33.501. The short answer for security teams is that NSSAA extends the operator's trust boundary to a third-party authentication server, so its security depends as much on the AAA infrastructure, the EAP method and the integration design as on the 5G core itself. This article explains how NSSAA works, what the Network Slice-Specific Authentication and Authorization Function (NSSAAF) does, how NSSAA differs from secondary authentication, and which risks operators should assess before offering slices to enterprises, public safety agencies or industrial customers. It goes one level deeper than our overview of security challenges in 5G network slicing , which covers isolation and orchestration rather than slice access control. What NSSAA is and why it exists NSSAA is an additional, per-slice authentication step that a network can require before it allows a UE to use a specific S-NSSAI. Primary authentication with 5G-AKA or EAP-AKA' proves that the subscriber is who the operator thinks it is; NSSAA proves that the slice owner also accepts that subscriber on its slice. The requirement came from slice-as-a-service models. An enterprise, a utility or a government agency that buys a dedicated slice often wants to control who joins it, using credentials it already manages, without handing that control to the mobile operator. NSSAA meets that need by letting the operator relay an EAP exchange between the device and an AAA server that belongs to the slice owner. The operator still authenticates the subscriber; the tenant authorises access to its slice. Whether a given S-NSSAI requires NSSAA is part of the subscription data held in the UDM. When a UE requests such a slice, the network does not include it in the Allowed NSSAI straight away. The slice stays pending until NSSAA succeeds, and is rejected if it fails. How the NSSAA procedure works NSSAA is an EAP exchange in which the AMF acts as the EAP authenticator, the NSSAAF acts as the bridge to the AAA world, and the AAA server of the slice owner (the AAA-S) makes the decision. The procedure is described in TS 23.502 and the NSSAAF service is defined in TS 29.526. The main steps are as follows: The UE registers and completes primary authentication. The AMF learns from subscription data that one or more requested S-NSSAIs are subject to NSSAA. The AMF sends a NAS Network Slice-Specific Authentication Command to the UE, carrying the S-NSSAI and an EAP identity request. The UE answers with an EAP identity response for that slice.
