英文原标题：N3IWF security in 5G: how untrusted Wi-Fi access reaches the core and where it breaks

The N3IWF is the function that lets a device on an untrusted Wi-Fi network reach the 5G core, terminating an IKEv2 and IPsec tunnel from the handset on one side and the N2 and N3 interfaces of the core on the other. That makes it one of the very few 5G network functions deliberately exposed to the public internet, which is why it deserves the same attention as a SEPP rather than being filed under Wi-Fi calling and forgotten. Its security model is defined in 3GPP TS 33.501, with the architecture in TS 23.501 and the device-facing procedures in TS 24.502, and the interesting part is not the cryptography, which is standard IETF work, but how much behaviour 3GPP leaves to operator configuration. Anyone who has assessed an ePDG will recognise the shape of the problem, because the ePDG plays the equivalent role for 4G and VoWiFi. The N3IWF is not simply a renamed ePDG, though. Authentication is done by tunnelling 5G NAS inside a vendor-specific EAP method, so the decision happens at the AMF and the AUSF rather than at the gateway, and the gateway relays NAS traffic it cannot read. This article covers how a device reaches the core over untrusted access, what EAP-5G does, where the N3IWF differs from the ePDG operationally, what the exposed attack surface looks like, and how to test and monitor it. What the N3IWF is and why 5G needed one The N3IWF, or Non-3GPP InterWorking Function, is the 5G core's gateway for access networks that 3GPP does not control and does not trust. In practice that means Wi-Fi, and in most deployments it means Wi-Fi calling and Wi-Fi data offload for handsets that are already 5G subscribers. 3GPP separates non-3GPP access into trusted and untrusted categories, and the distinction is about who operates the access network rather than about the strength of its encryption. An untrusted access network is any network the operator has no control over, which includes home broadband, hotel Wi-Fi and public hotspots. Because the operator cannot make assumptions about that path, the security model does not try to secure the access network. It secures the tunnel that runs across it, end to end between the device and the N3IWF, and treats everything in between as hostile. That is the same decision 3GPP made for the ePDG in the evolved packet core. Architecturally the N3IWF sits in an unusual position. On the device side it terminates the NWu reference point, carrying IKEv2 and IPsec from the handset. On the core side it terminates N2 toward the AMF and N3 toward the UPF, exactly as a gNB does. From the core's perspective it is an access node. From the internet's perspective it is a public IKEv2 responder. Those two facts together are the whole security story, because the function has to be reachable by anything on the internet while speaking the same interfaces as the radio access network.
